Engineering specification v2.0 — evidence-based 5 September 2026

Al-Mushtabah Engine

An Arabic deduction game on a grid of up to 16×16. This document defines the rules, the data model, the solver, the generator, and the production pipeline. Every claim below was tested before it was written.

Headline finding

Generation works at 16×16. We do not need anyone else's puzzles.

The open question was whether we could generate valid large puzzles ourselves, or whether we would be forced to hand-author every one. Two prototypes failed before the third succeeded — and the failures were informative, not fatal. A propagation-based generator now produces solvable, uniquely-determined 16×16 cases at a 100% success rate across 15 consecutive runs.

The decisive insight came from reverse-engineering Murdoku, the closest existing product: its puzzles are solvable by pure constraint propagation — no guessing, no backtracking. That means we never have to count solutions in a space of 4.4 × 1026. We only have to run inference to a fixed point and check that every character is pinned. That is fast, exact, and it produces the player hint chain for free.

01

What was verified, and how

This section exists so nothing downstream rests on assumption. Each row is a claim that was executed, not estimated.

Brute force is impossible above ~7×7

Each character occupies a unique row and a unique column, so a placement is a pair of permutations: n! × n! possibilities.

GridPlacementsVerdict
5 × 514,400Exhaustive search is instant
7 × 725,401,600Practical ceiling
10 × 101.3 × 1013Out of reach
16 × 164.4 × 1026Impossible by counting

One clue per character does not scale

Tested with randomly generated 4-room layouts, selecting the single most restrictive true clue for each character, then counting solutions exactly. Median over 15 trials per size:

GridSolutions foundVerdict
5 × 56Not unique
6 × 624Not unique
8 × 81,152Not unique
10 × 10172,800Not unique
16 × 16> 2,000,000Hopeless

This is why the naive design fails. It is also why simple per-character clue cards are not the whole story in any shipping product of this kind.

Propagation solves 16×16 reliably

Given the full set of true clues, a constraint propagator reached a complete unique assignment in 23 of 23 generated 16×16 layouts. Zero contradictions, zero stalls.

The generator produces minimal clue sets

Correct algorithm: start from all true clues (guaranteed solvable), then remove clues one by one for as long as propagation still solves the puzzle. Every surviving clue is therefore necessary.

GridRoomsSuccessFinal cluesMax per character
8 × 8722 / 2510.54
12 × 121015 / 1516.93
16 × 161415 / 1524.95

Calibration. Murdoku's 16×16 zoo case ships 16 cards carrying roughly 20 atomic facts. Our untuned generator produces about 25. We are already in the right range, and clue-type weighting will close the gap.

Two prototypes failed first — recorded so we don't repeat them

AttemptWhy it failed
Exhaustive countingNo global rules modelled; search space unbounded above 7×7.
Incremental clue addingAdding clues greedily and testing after each one gets trapped in local minima. Removal beats addition — start from a solvable state and subtract.
02

Game rules

The grid is not an elimination table. It is a map of the scene, divided into rooms, holding furniture and objects. The player places characters onto the map.

RuleDefinition
One per row and columnEach row holds exactly one character; so does each column. Placing a character eliminates its row and column for everyone else.
RoomsThe grid is partitioned into named regions. Rooms scope adjacency — "beside" never crosses a wall.
BesideDirectly left, right, above or below, and in the same room. Diagonals do not count.
The victimOne character is the victim. Their card always reads: alone with the killer — meaning their room holds exactly two people.
The killerNever asked directly. It is whoever ends up in the victim's room. The answer emerges from the completed map.

Clue catalogue

Every clue must be machine-checkable: the engine must decide true or false for any placement. Anything that cannot be expressed here does not ship.

TypeArgumentsExample wording
inRoomchar, room"He was in the storeroom."
notInRoomchar, room"She was not in the kitchen."
onObjectchar, object"He was at the well."
notOnObjectchar, object"She was not on the carpet."
besideObjectchar, object"She was beside the table."
notBesideObjectchar, object"He was not beside an animal."
besideCharchar, char"He was beside Khalid."
sameRoomchar, char"They shared a room."
diffRoomchar, char"They were not in the same room."
rowOffsetchar, n, char"He was exactly one row north of Ivan."
inRow / inColchar, n"She was in the second column."
aloneInRoomchar"He was alone."
aloneWithchar, char"She was alone with Mara."

Rejected clue type: anything referencing a dimension the grid does not model. "He arrived before her" implies time; there is no time axis, so the player cannot deduce it and the engine cannot check it. Either add time as a real dimension or drop the clue class entirely.

04

Global rules — the key to large grids

Per-character clues alone cannot pin a 16×16 grid. What makes large boards tractable are rules that constrain every character simultaneously. Murdoku stores these as numeric ids into a fixed catalogue (clue_0, clue_1); in its zoo case they resolve to a class restriction and a covering constraint. Removing them from our own generator was exactly what made it fail.

RuleEffectArabic framing
classRestrictionCharacters of class B may never enter rooms of type R. Prunes many domains at once.Household vs guests · staff vs customers · workers vs owners
noEmptyRegionEvery restricted room holds at least one character. Forbids empty rooms and drives most of the deduction chain."Every wing had someone on duty."
regionQuotaA named region holds exactly k people."Three of them were in the courtyard."
pairwiseClassNo two characters of the same class share a room."No two guards stood together."

Global rules are the cheapest information in the system: one sentence for the player, an enormous reduction for the solver. Every case above 8×8 should carry at least two.

One self-contained JSON file per case, plus thin per-language overlays. The overlay pattern is worth copying directly — it is why a competitor supports 23 languages at negligible cost.

Two decisions worth locking now. First, roomMap as a flat row-major array rather than per-cell keys — it is a tenth of the size and trivially iterable. Second, solution as explicit [row, col] pairs rather than pixel coordinates; Murdoku stores pixels and needs division to recover the grid, which is fragile and leaks the answer to anyone who reads the file.

06

The engine

Two components. Get these right and the rest is interface.

A — The propagator

Not a counter. It maintains a domain of possible cells per character and applies inference rules to a fixed point. If every domain collapses to one cell, the puzzle is valid and solvable without guessing.

// each rule returns true if it changed any domain
function propagate(puzzle, domains) {
  let changed = true;
  while (changed) {
    changed = false;
    changed |= applyUnaryClues(domains);      // inRoom, besideObject, …
    changed |= eliminateRowsAndCols(domains); // a placed char clears its row + column
    changed |= hiddenSingles(domains);        // only one char can occupy row r
    changed |= aloneConstraints(domains);     // alone / aloneWith clear their room
    changed |= noEmptyRegion(domains);        // a room that must be occupied
    if (anyDomainEmpty(domains)) return { ok: false };
  }
  return { ok: true, solved: domains.every(d => d.size === 1) };
}

B — The generator

The ordering matters more than anything else in this document. Start complete, then subtract. Adding clues incrementally was tested and fails; it stalls in local minima.

function generate(layout) {
  const solution = randomPlacementSatisfying(layout.globalRules);
  let clues = allTrueClues(solution, layout);   // hundreds; solvable by construction

  shuffle(clues);
  for (const clue of [...clues]) {              // try to drop each one
    const trial = clues.filter(c => c !== clue);
    if (propagate(layout, domainsFrom(trial)).solved) clues = trial;
  }
  // every surviving clue is necessary: removing it breaks solvability
  return { ...layout, solution, clues, hintChain: traceOf(clues) };
}

C — Difficulty, measured not guessed

Clue count is a poor proxy. Grade by which inference rules the propagator needed:

TierCriterion
EasySolved by direct elimination and naked singles only.
MediumRequires hidden singles across rows or columns.
HardRequires room-occupancy reasoning — alone, aloneWith, region quotas.
ExpertRequires chained global-rule deductions; the hint chain runs past six steps.

D — The hint chain comes free

Record every propagation step and you have the walkthrough. Two verbs cover it: block (eliminate these cells, for this reason) and isolate (this character can only be here). Murdoku ships seven such hints per expert case and appears to write them by hand — we generate ours from the trace.

07

Map art direction

The brief is photoreal 3D, not flat colour blocks. That collides with a hard requirement: this is a logic game, and the player must be able to read the grid precisely. Resolving that collision is the whole job of this section.

The app is 2D. Only the asset pipeline is 3D.

This distinction matters more than it sounds. We render tiles offline in Blender and ship the results as flat sprites. At runtime the game draws images into grid cells — there is no 3D engine, no WebGL, no camera maths, no performance cliff on an old phone. All the realism is baked into the picture before it ever reaches the browser.

So "3D is hard" never becomes a problem in the code. It is only ever a question of how we produce the pictures, and that question has three answers, all of which look realistic and all of which the engine treats identically.

RouteHow the tiles are madeSkill neededLook
A — RenderedModelled and lit in Blender, exported as isometric sprites3D modellingPhotoreal, consistent lighting, best result
B — PaintedIllustrated top-down tiles, generated and then cleaned up by hand2D art direction onlyRealistic painted, warmer, stylised edge
C — TexturedReal material photographs as floors, prop cut-outs composited on topPhoto editingReal materials, flat top-down, fastest to produce

Architectural consequence — decide this late, not now. The renderer's contract is one line: given a cell, draw a sprite. Nothing in the engine, the data model, or the interaction layer knows which route produced that sprite. We can ship on route C, upgrade to route A a year later, and change no game code at all. Do not let the art decision block Phase 0.

Why pre-rendering beats real-time either way

Real-time Three.js buys camera freedom we do not need and costs quality, battery and complexity we cannot spare. A pre-rendered tile can carry an hour of ray-traced global illumination; a real-time frame gets sixteen milliseconds.

The one rule that makes or breaks it, whichever route you take: every tile must share an identical viewing angle and an identical light direction. Fix the projection and the sun azimuth before producing a single asset, and write them into the art brief. Tiles made at drifting angles never assemble into a coherent room, and redoing a library later costs more than building it did.

Two views of one map — the move that buys us both

Ship a toggle, not a compromise:

Scene view — default

  • Full isometric 3D, real materials, atmosphere and mood grade
  • Grid implied by subtle light-etched floor seams, not drawn lines
  • This is what the player screenshots and shares

Plan view — one tap away

  • Flattened top-down, high contrast, thin grid, every label legible
  • Made for the hard deduction phase and for printing
  • Same data, same coordinates — purely a rendering switch

Murdoku has one view and therefore had to stay flat and readable. Two views let us be fully realistic where realism sells the game, and fully legible where the player is actually thinking. This is the single strongest art-direction decision in the document.

Making 3D legible — four details that decide it

ProblemResolution
Which cell is that token on?Every occupied cell gets a faint floor decal ring under the figure. This is the most common failure in isometric games and it is fatal for a logic puzzle.
Tall props hide charactersPainter's algorithm sorted by row + col; any occluder fades to 60% while a token behind it is selected. Plus a 90° rotate control giving four viewing orientations.
Figures lost against busy floorsA rim light on every character render, and a soft darkening vignette on the cell beneath.
Which column was that?Sticky row and column headers that stay pinned while panning — non-negotiable when clues say "the second column" on a 16-wide board.

Variety without new assets

One tile library, four global colour grades — day, dusk, night, storm — plus per-case prop dressing. A courtyard at noon and the same courtyard at night read as different places for the cost of a grade.

Tooling: Claude Design for composition studies, UI layout and the case-file framing; Blender for the tile and character renders themselves.

08

Interaction model

Placing sixteen characters on a 256-cell board has to feel effortless, on a phone, with one thumb. The design below removes modes entirely and eliminates the long-press.

The core rule. The meaning of a tap is decided by one piece of state: whether a character is currently selected. Nothing else. No mode buttons, no gesture vocabulary to learn.

StateTapping a cell does
No character selectedToggles an elimination mark ✗ on that cell. This is the bulk activity of solving, so it gets the zero-friction path.
Character selectedCycles that cell through pencil → committed → clear.

Selecting a character

Tap the card. Four things happen at once, and all four are information:

  • The card lifts and its clue text expands to full length.
  • Cells still legal for that character glow softly — optional, since it is a real hint.
  • If already placed, their token pulses once so you can find them on a crowded board.
  • Any clue card referencing them gains a connecting tint.

Tap the card again, or tap outside the board, to deselect. On desktop: number and letter keys select, arrows move a cursor, Enter commits, Esc deselects.

Feedback on commit — show the consequence, not just the result

The token drops with its shadow contracting under it and a soft landing sound. Then the automatic eliminations cascade outward along the row and column at roughly 40 ms per cell, rather than appearing all at once. The player watches their own deduction propagate. This is the single most satisfying moment in the loop and it deserves the animation budget.

Guard rails that never spoil

If a commit contradicts a clue the player has already been shown, the offending clue card pulses red and the cell shakes briefly. The move is still allowed — never block the player, never take the pen out of their hand. The check runs against stated clues only and never consults the solution, so it cannot leak the answer.

Explicitly rejected: long-press as a primary action (slow, undiscoverable, conflicts with system text selection), drag-and-drop as the only placement route (exhausting across 256 cells on a phone), and any hidden gesture. Drag remains available as an alternative for players who reach for it, never as the required path.

Sixteen clue cards on a small screen

The real layout problem of the expert tier. The resolution: a horizontally swipeable strip pinned above the board, expandable to a full list sheet. Cards sort themselves — unresolved first, struck ones grey and sink. Each card shows the character portrait, name, and clue, and grows on selection to show the full text without truncation. A card never clips its own sentence; if it cannot fit, it scrolls inside itself.

Accessibility and feel

  • Marks are distinguished by shape, not colour alone — the ✗ and the pencil letter differ in form.
  • Full keyboard play: select, move, commit, undo, toggle view.
  • prefers-reduced-motion collapses the cascade to an instant state change.
  • Screen reader announces cell coordinates, room name, and occupant on focus.
  • Haptic tick on every mark, a heavier one on commit; sound independently mutable.
  • Text scale respected throughout; clue cards reflow rather than truncate.
  • State autosaves continuously — closing the tab mid-case never loses work.
10

Production pipeline

  1. Draw the layout

    Rooms and their walls, objects inside them, blocked cells. Target roughly 0.85 rooms per grid row — 14 rooms on a 16×16. Small rooms make room clues powerful.

  2. Define characters and classes

    Names, avatar parameters, and the class split the global rules will use. Mark the victim.

  3. Run the generator

    It picks a solution satisfying the global rules, then reduces to a minimal necessary clue set.

  4. Check the gate

    Propagation must reach a full unique assignment with no guessing. Anything else goes back to step 3.

  5. Write the Arabic

    Turn machine clues into natural sentences. Meaning must not shift — only voice. This is where dialect and character enter.

  6. Play it as a human

    One person solves it unaided and reaches the same killer. The generated hint chain is the reference for where they should have got stuck.

  7. Publish

    Master file plus language overlays, added to the catalogue with its measured difficulty tier.

11

Where we differ

The mechanic is not the product. Four directions that are ours, verified as absent from the reference implementation.

The lying witness

  • Exactly one card is false — the killer's.
  • Machine-checkable: the puzzle has a unique solution only when that one card is ignored.
  • Turns pure deduction into actual investigation.
  • Not present in Murdoku.

Time as a second layer

  • Same grid, two snapshots: before and after.
  • Unlocks movement clues — "he left the majlis for the kitchen".
  • Doubles depth without enlarging the board.
  • Also legitimises the "arrived before" clue class we had to reject.

Arabic global rules

  • Household vs guests, staff vs customers, workers vs owners.
  • Identical mathematical power, entirely different world.
  • Names, rooms and objects drawn from a Saudi setting.

Architectural rendering

  • Murdoku uses flat colour blocks with top-down sprites.
  • Ours: a line-drawn floor plan — thin cell grid, heavy wall strokes, hatched regions.
  • Reads as a case file rather than a board game, and stays legible in print.

13

Acceptance criteria

No case ships until all of these hold. This list is the last line of defence before a broken puzzle reaches a player.

  • Propagation reaches a complete assignment — every character pinned to exactly one cell.
  • No guessing or backtracking was required at any step.
  • Every clue is necessary: removing any one of them breaks solvability.
  • Every clue references a dimension the grid actually models.
  • The victim's room contains exactly two people, so the killer is unambiguous.
  • The Arabic wording matches the machine clue exactly — no added or lost meaning.
  • The generated hint chain reads as a sensible human argument end to end.
  • At least one person solved it unaided and named the same killer.
14

Reference boundary

Murdoku was studied closely and the analysis above is drawn from its public files. The line between study and appropriation is worth stating plainly, because it also happens to be the practical line.

Adopted — ideas and structure

  • Static delivery: master file plus thin language overlays
  • Cell-indexed room maps and procedural avatars
  • Global rules addressed by id from a catalogue
  • Propagation-solvability as the publication gate
  • A hint chain in block / isolate vocabulary

Not taken — authored work

  • Their puzzle instances: layouts, solutions, clue sets
  • Their hint and story text
  • Their SVG art and character designs
  • Their room and character names

The practical argument, independent of anything else. Their map is their puzzle — every clue points at specific cells in a specific layout. Redraw the layout and every clue breaks; keep the layout and nothing has actually changed but the icons. There is no version of reskinning that both works and is different. A generator is required either way, and as of this document it exists and succeeds 15 times out of 15 at 16×16.
